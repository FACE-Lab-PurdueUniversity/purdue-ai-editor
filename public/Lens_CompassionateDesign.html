Compassionate Design

A lens for designing with people who are often stigmatized, vulnerable, or overlooked — asking not just whether a design works, but whether it protects someone's dignity while it does.

The Big Idea Three Considerations Foundational Cases In the Course Self-Check
The Big Idea

Function isn't enough when dignity is on the line.

Compassionate Design, as framed by Seshadri, Joslyn, Hynes & Reid (2019), looks at products built for people in difficult or stigmatized circumstances — amputees, refugees, sick children, people experiencing poverty — and asks what separates the designs that quietly humiliate the people they're meant to help from the ones that restore some control and self-respect. Two products can solve the same technical problem and land completely differently on this scale.

Three Considerations

Dignity, empowerment, sense of security.

These are the three qualities the framework asks you to protect or restore, especially for people who have little say in what gets designed for them.

01

Dignity

Does the design treat the person as a full, capable person — or does it visibly mark them as a problem to be managed? Appearance and framing matter as much as function.

Would you be comfortable using this in public, exactly as it looks?
02

Empowerment

Does the design give someone more control over their own life and choices, or does it make decisions for them? Compassionate design hands agency back.

Who is doing the deciding once this is in someone's hands?
03

Sense of Security

Does the design account for the specific risks and fears of the situation it's used in — physical safety, social exposure, emotional distress — not just the average case?

What is this person actually afraid of, and does the design address it?

Jaipur Foot
Jaipur Foot
ProblemAn affordable prosthetic for amputees in regions where walking barefoot, squatting, and sitting cross-legged are part of daily life — needs Western prosthetics don't meet.
ResponseA low-cost, flexible prosthetic foot shaped and weighted to move and look like a natural bare foot.
Why CompassionateRestores culturally specific mobility and appearance, not just a generic definition of "walking."
Image coming

Berkeley Darfur Stove
Berkeley Darfur Stove
ProblemTraditional three-stone fires in refugee camps required long firewood-gathering trips that exposed women to serious danger.
ResponseA fuel-efficient, engineered cookstove that sharply cuts how much firewood a household needs.
Why CompassionateReduces exposure to a specific, named danger, not just fuel cost — the safety of the person, not only the efficiency of the stove.

GE MRI for Kids
GE MRI for Kids
ProblemMRI machines frighten young children badly enough that many need sedation just to complete a scan.
ResponseThe room and machine are redecorated as a themed adventure, with staff guiding the child through it as a story rather than a procedure.
Why CompassionateAddresses the child's emotional experience directly — the scanner's technical function didn't change, but the person's experience of it did.

Self-Check

Applying it to your own work.

Three questions to ask about anything you're building, especially if it's meant for someone in a vulnerable or difficult situation.

Dignity

Does this mark the person using it as someone with a problem, or does it treat them as a capable person, full stop?

Empowerment

Does this give the person more control over their situation, or does it quietly take decisions away from them?

Sense of Security

What is this person specifically afraid of or at risk from — and does the design actually address that, or just the average case?

Source

Seshadri, Joslyn, Hynes & Reid, "Compassionate Design" (2019).
